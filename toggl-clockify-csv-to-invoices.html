Turn a Toggl or Clockify CSV into client invoices (free script, with rounding and tax done right)

2026-09-21

If you track time in Toggl, Clockify, Harvest or a spreadsheet, month-end invoicing is a mechanical job that still eats an hour: group by client, round, multiply by the rate, add tax, number the invoice, work out the due date. It is also a job where small arithmetic slips (a cent here, a rounded-down quarter hour there) quietly cost you or annoy a client. Here is how to do it properly, and a free script that does it.

The steps

  1. Export the month with at least date, client, hours (and ideally project and description, which become the invoice lines).
  2. Filter to the billing month. Entries outside it belong on another invoice.
  3. Apply your rounding policy to each entry (see below).
  4. Multiply by the client's rate and round each line to the cent.
  5. Add up the lines, apply any minimum charge, then add tax on the subtotal and round to the cent.
  6. Number the invoice sequentially and set the due date from the client's payment terms.

Round each entry, or round the total?

Rounding each entry up to the next 15 minutes (0.25 h) is common, and it bills more than rounding the monthly total: ten 5-minute tasks become 2.5 hours rather than about 50 minutes. Some clients accept that, others object. Whichever you choose, put it in your contract or proposal, apply it the same way to everyone, and never round down silently on entries you intend to bill. Both approaches are legitimate; hidden ones are not.

Money arithmetic: use decimals, not floats

In many languages 0.1 + 0.2 is not exactly 0.3, which is why money should be computed with decimal types and rounded once, at a defined point (half-up to the cent is the usual rule). A line of 1.75 hours at 70.00 is exactly 122.50; a tax rate of 21% on 2,145.00 is 450.45 after rounding. The same input should always produce the same invoice.

Minimum charges and tax

If a client has a minimum monthly charge (say 150.00) and the month's time comes to 122.50, the subtotal becomes 150.00 and then tax applies (20% is 30.00), for a total of 180.00. Applying tax first and the minimum after gives a different, wrong-looking total. Tax rules differ by country and by client, so use the rate that applies to you and check the details where you operate.

Numbering

Use sequential invoice numbers with no gaps, and continue from the last one you issued. Many jurisdictions expect it and your own bookkeeping will thank you.
