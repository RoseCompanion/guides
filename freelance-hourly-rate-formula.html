How to calculate your freelance hourly rate (formula, worked example, and a script)

2026-09-21

Most freelancers set their rate by copying an employee salary divided by 2,080 hours. That number is too low, because you do not bill 2,080 hours: you take time off, you spend a large share of your working week on things you cannot bill (finding clients, admin, invoicing, learning), and you carry costs an employer would have paid. Here is a formula that accounts for those.

The formula

billable hours per year = (52 - weeks off) x working hours per week x utilisation
revenue needed          = (target income + business expenses) / (1 - set-aside)
minimum hourly rate     = revenue needed / billable hours per year
  • Utilisation is the share of your working hours that you can actually bill. 50-70% is common for freelancers; you should use your own tracked number if you have one.
  • Set-aside is a percentage you choose to hold back for tax, benefits or savings. It depends on where you live and your situation, so this is your number, not a rule.

Worked example

Target take-home 60,000, business expenses 6,000, 6 weeks off, 35 hours a week, 60% utilisation, no set-aside:

  • Billable hours: (52 - 6) x 35 x 0.60 = 966 hours
  • Revenue needed: 60,000 + 6,000 = 66,000
  • Minimum hourly rate: 66,000 / 966 = 68.32
  • With 4.2 billable hours in an average working day, that is a day rate of about 286.96

Now see how sensitive it is:

  • If you set aside 25% for tax and savings, revenue needed becomes 88,000 and the minimum hourly rate becomes 91.10.
  • If you could raise utilisation to 80%, billable hours become 1,288 and the minimum hourly rate falls to 51.24.

Improving utilisation is often a bigger lever than negotiating harder, and the formula shows why undercharging is so easy: the hours you can bill are far fewer than the hours you work.

What this number is and is not

It is a floor: the rate below which the business does not meet your own targets. It is not a price. What you charge should also reflect the value of the work to the client, what the market pays for your skills and experience, and how much demand you have. If you are consistently fully booked at your floor, it is probably too low.

A copy-paste snippet

from decimal import Decimal as D
def min_rate(income, expenses, weeks_off=6, hours_per_week=35, utilisation=0.60, set_aside=0.0):
    hours = (52 - weeks_off) * hours_per_week * D(str(utilisation))
    revenue = (D(income) + D(expenses)) / (1 - D(str(set_aside)))
    return round(revenue / hours, 2)

print(min_rate(60000, 6000))          # 68.32
print(min_rate(60000, 6000, set_aside=0.25))  # 91.10
